## Q1: "How much house can I afford on a $100k salary?"
A common rule: keep total housing costs under 28% of gross monthly income. On $100k that's ~$2,333/month for PITI (principal, interest, taxes, insurance). At 6.5% on a 30-year loan with 20% down, that lands around a $380k–$420k home depending on your local property taxes.

But the 28% rule is just a starting point — your debts matter too (the 36% back-end rule). Rather than guessing, punch your actual numbers into a home affordability calculator — I built a free one that factors income, debts, down payment, rate, taxes and insurance together: https://www.theutilityhub.online/tools/home-affordability-calculator

Biggest mistake I see: people budget the mortgage payment but forget property tax + insurance can add 30-40% on top.

## Q2: "Debt snowball vs avalanche — which pays off debt faster?"
Mathematically, avalanche (highest interest first) always wins — you pay less interest, so you're debt-free sooner. On typical balances the difference is a few hundred dollars and 1-3 months.

But snowball (smallest balance first) wins *behaviorally* for most people — wiping out a whole debt in 2-3 months gives you a win that keeps you going, and most payoff plans fail from quitting, not from math.

My take: if the interest-rate gap between your debts is small (<3%), do snowball for the motivation. If one debt is 24% APR and the rest are 8%, do avalanche — the math gap is too big to ignore.

## Q3: "How does compound interest actually work? Can you show a real example?"
Simple version: you earn returns not just on your money, but on your previous returns too — so growth accelerates.

Real example: invest $500/month at 7% average annual return.
- After 10 years: ~$86,000 ($60k of it your contributions)
- After 20 years: ~$262,000 ($120k contributions)
- After 30 years: ~$613,000 ($180k contributions)

Notice the pattern: in the first decade you did most of the work; in the last decade compounding did most of it. That's why starting 10 years earlier beats earning a higher return.

One caveat: 7% is a long-term stock-market average, not a promise — real years bounce around a lot.
