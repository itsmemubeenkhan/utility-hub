If you are asking **should you cosign a student loan**, here is the short answer: only if you can afford to repay the whole loan yourself, because legally that is what you are agreeing to do. A cosigner is not a character reference. You are equally responsible for every payment, every late fee, and the full balance if the student stops paying. For a typical private loan of **$40,000 at 8% over 10 years, that is $485.31 a month and $18,237.25 in interest**, and the lender can come to you for any of it, at any point, without exhausting its options against the student first.

This matters because cosigning is extremely common: about 9 in 10 undergraduate private student loans carry a cosigner, usually a parent. Federal Direct student loans are different, they need no cosigner and no credit check, so a cosigner request almost always means a private loan, which has fewer safety nets than a federal one.

## What cosigning actually means

When you cosign, three things happen at once:

- **Full liability.** You owe 100% of the debt, not half. If the balance is $40,000, your exposure is $40,000 plus interest and fees.
- **Credit reporting.** The loan appears on your credit report as your debt, counts toward your debt-to-income ratio for future borrowing, and any missed payment damages your score too.
- **Collection rights.** If payments stop, the lender can pursue you directly, including through collections and, depending on the contract and state law, legal action.

## The real monthly cost by loan size

Private loan payments at 8% on the standard 10-year plan:

| Loan balance | Monthly payment | Total interest | Total paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| $20,000 | $242.66 | $9,118.62 | $29,118.62 |
| $40,000 | $485.31 | $18,237.25 | $58,237.25 |
| $60,000 | $727.97 | $27,355.87 | $87,355.87 |

Stretching the $40,000 loan to 15 years drops the payment to $382.26 a month, but total interest jumps to $28,806.95, which is $10,569.70 more than the 10-year plan. A lower payment is not a lower cost.

Check the debt-to-income effect too. That $485.31 payment is 9.7% of a $5,000 monthly gross income and 11.6% of $4,166.67 (a $50,000 salary), enough to change a mortgage decision. See [what is debt-to-income ratio](/blog/what-is-debt-to-income-ratio) for how lenders add it up.

## What happens if the student misses payments

This is the scenario every cosigner should price in before signing:

- **Interest keeps running.** One month of interest on a $40,000 loan at 8% is $266.67, charged whether or not anyone pays. Three missed months add roughly $800 in interest alone.
- **Fees stack up.** A typical late fee of 5% adds about $24.26 to a $485.31 payment, and it repeats.
- **Your credit takes the hit.** Late payments are reported on your file as well as the student's, and a serious delinquency can stay on both reports for years.
- **You can be pursued first.** Many private loan contracts let the lender collect from either signer, so you cannot insist the lender chase the student first.

Private loans follow the contract, and the contract is usually strict.

## Cosigner release: real, but not automatic

Some private lenders offer cosigner release, typically after 24 to 48 consecutive on-time payments, but release is never guaranteed:

- The student must apply for it, and must then qualify for the loan alone, with enough income and credit history.
- If the student's income or credit is still weak, the application is denied and you stay on the loan.
- Some lenders do not offer release at all, so check the contract before signing, not after.

If release matters to you, get the exact conditions in writing: how many on-time payments, what income or credit standard applies, and whether one late payment resets the clock. Do not rely on a verbal promise.

## When cosigning can make sense, and when it should not

Cosigning can be reasonable when the loan is modest, you could cover the payment without hardship, the student has a realistic repayment plan, and the lender offers a clear release path. It is a bad idea when the payment would strain your own budget, when you are near retirement and cannot absorb a decade of liability, when the student is borrowing far more than their expected starting salary, or when the loan would block your own mortgage or refinance.

## Alternatives to cosigning

Try these before anyone signs:

1. **Max out federal loans first.** Direct loans need no cosigner, and they come with income-driven repayment and forgiveness options private loans do not.
2. **Borrow less.** A cheaper school, two years at a community college, or a part-time job can cut the private loan to zero, the only risk-free option.
3. **Compare lenders as a pair.** Some offer lower rates or better release terms than others. Run each offer through our [loan comparison calculator](/tools/loan-comparison-calculator) before choosing.
4. **Get the release terms in writing.** If you do cosign, a written release path with a date to revisit it is worth more than a lower rate.
5. **Build a backup plan.** Agree in advance who pays if the student cannot, and for how long, so a missed payment never surprises either of you.

If you do end up covering payments, paying extra helps both of you. Adding $200 a month to the $40,000 loan at 8% clears it in 75 months instead of 120 and saves $7,404.78 in interest. Even $100 extra clears it in 92 months and saves $4,672.67.

## FAQ

### Should you cosign a student loan?
Only if you can afford to repay the full loan yourself, because you are legally equally responsible for it. If the payment would strain your budget, or you are counting on the student never missing a payment, do not sign.

### What does it mean to cosign a student loan?
It means you promise to repay the loan if the student does not. The full balance appears on your credit report, counts toward your debt-to-income ratio, and the lender can collect from you directly, including through collections.

### How much does a cosigned student loan cost the cosigner?
Whatever the payment is. On a $40,000 private loan at 8% over 10 years, that is $485.31 a month and $18,237.25 in interest. On $60,000 at the same terms, it is $727.97 a month and $27,355.87 in interest.

### Can a cosigner be removed from a student loan?
Sometimes, through cosigner release, typically after 24 to 48 on-time payments and only if the student then qualifies alone. Release is not automatic, must be applied for, and some lenders do not offer it at all. Check the contract before signing.

### Does cosigning a student loan affect the cosigner's credit?
Yes. The loan appears on your credit report, raises your debt-to-income ratio for future borrowing, and any late or missed payment damages your score just as it damages the student's.

### What are the alternatives to cosigning a student loan?
Federal Direct loans first (no cosigner needed), borrowing less, choosing a cheaper school, comparing private lenders for better release terms, and agreeing a written backup plan for who pays if the student cannot.
