$100,000 is the six-figure milestone - above the US median and enough for a comfortable home in most of the country. If you are wondering **how much house can I afford making $100,000 a year**, the lending industry's standard **28/36 rule** puts the answer around **$340k–$385k**, depending on your debts, down payment, rate, and state. Here is the exact math.

## The 28/36 rule, step by step

Lenders use two ratios:

- **Front-end (28%):** Housing costs ≤ 28% of gross monthly income
- **Back-end (36%):** All monthly debts ≤ 36% of gross monthly income

On $100,000/year ($8,333/month):

- Front-end limit: $8,333 × 0.28 = **$2,333/month** for housing
- Back-end limit: $8,333 × 0.36 = $3,000/month for everything

If you have $600/month in car and student loan payments, the back-end rule allows $3,000 − $600 = $2,400 for housing, so the front-end $2,333 is the binding limit.

## Converting the payment to a price

At 6.5% on a 30-year loan, $2,333/month in principal and interest supports a loan of about **$369,000**. With 20% down, that means a home price of roughly **$461,000**, but wait. That $2,333 must also cover property taxes and insurance (PITI), which lenders include in "housing." After ~$400/month for taxes and insurance, the P&I budget is closer to $1,933, supporting a loan of ~$306,000 and a price of **~$385,000** with 20% down.

Realistic ranges on $100K salary:

| Down payment | Approx. max home price (6.5%, 30-yr) |
|---|---|
| 3% down (conventional) | ~$325,000 |
| 10% down | ~$340,000 |
| 20% down | ~$385,000 |
| Debts over $1,000/mo | subtract ~$60,000 |

## Five things that move the number

1. **Interest rate.** At 5.5% instead of 6.5%, the same payment buys ~12% more house - about $42,000 extra at this income. On a six-figure salary, refinancing when rates drop is serious money.
2. **Existing debts.** Every $200/month in debt payments cuts roughly $30,000 off your max price. At $100K income, you have the cash flow to eliminate a car payment in months, not years - do it before house hunting.
3. **Down payment.** 20% ($68,000–$77,000) avoids PMI (about $130–$255/month at this loan size) and is very achievable on $100K within 2–3 years of focused saving.
4. **Property taxes.** In New Jersey or Illinois, taxes can add $750+/month on a $365K home; in Colorado or Alabama, under $200. Location changes affordability enormously.
5. **HOA dues.** A $325/month HOA fee counts against your ratios exactly like a higher mortgage payment - always factor it into the "real" monthly cost.

## The costs buyers forget

The mortgage is only the headline. Budget ~1% of the home's value per year for maintenance ($3,650/year on a $365,000 home), plus utilities ($250–$400/month for a house), and an emergency fund covering 3–6 months of the *new* higher housing cost. Six figures does not make you immune to a $15,000 roof surprise - the emergency fund scales with the house.

## Should you max it out?

The 28/36 rule is a **ceiling**, not a target. At $100K, the comfortable zone for most buyers is $320K–$360K - leaving room for retirement contributions, investing, and actually enjoying the six-figure life. Maxing to $385K is doable but leaves you "house comfortable" rather than "house poor" only if the rest of your finances are tight.

**Where $100K buys comfortably:** Atlanta, Dallas, Phoenix, Denver suburbs, Chicago suburbs, Raleigh, Nashville.
**Where it is tight:** NYC, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Boston, Seattle, San Diego - expect condos or longer commutes.

## State-by-state: Texas, Florida, California

**Texas ($100K salary):** No state income tax, but property taxes at 1.6–2.2% mean $460–$630/month in taxes on a $345K home. Realistic range: **$325K–$365K**. Excellent buying power in all major Texas metros.

**Florida ($100K salary):** No state income tax, property tax ~0.9% (~$260/month on $345K), but homeowners insurance $200–$400/month and rising. Realistic range: **$335K–$375K**.

**California ($100K salary):** State income tax ~8.5% at this level hurts, and $100K is below what single-family homes require in coastal metros (median $700K+). Realistic range: **$340K–$385K** mathematically - practically, a condo inland or a dual-income purchase near job centers.

## FAQ

### How much house can I afford making $100,000 a year?
On a $100,000 salary with typical debts and 10–20% down, you can afford a home priced around **$340,000–$385,000** at current rates, using the 28/36 lending rule. That assumes roughly $2,333/month for total housing costs.

### What mortgage payment can I afford on $100K salary?
About **$2,333/month** for all housing costs (mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA) under the 28% front-end rule, assuming your total debts stay under 36% of income ($3,000/month).

### Can I afford a $400K house on a $100K salary?
It is a stretch. With 20% down ($80,000) and minimal debts, it can work - PITI runs about $2,620/month at 6.5%, slightly over the 28% guideline. With 10% down, it is over budget for most lenders.

### Is $100K a good salary to buy a house in 2026?
Yes - $100K is a strong buying income in most US markets, covering a 3–4 bedroom home in the South, Midwest, and suburbs. In the most expensive coastal cities, it typically means a condo or buying with a partner.

### How much house can I afford on $100K salary in Texas?
No state income tax helps, but property taxes (1.6–2.2%) take a bite. Realistically **$325K–$365K** with 10–20% down.
