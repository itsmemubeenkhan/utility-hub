The **Roth vs traditional 401(k)** choice looks complicated, but it comes down to one bet: will your tax rate be higher now or in retirement? Pick correctly and the math can put thousands of extra dollars in your pocket. Pick wrong and you hand that money to the IRS. Here is the decision with real numbers, including exactly what each option costs your paycheck.

## The one-line answer

If your tax rate stays the same from now until retirement, Roth and traditional 401(k) give you **exactly the same** spendable money. The entire decision is a bet on whether your future tax rate will be higher (choose Roth) or lower (choose traditional) than today's.

## The $10,000 test: why it is a tie at equal tax rates

Take $10,000 of pre-tax income, a 22% tax bracket now and in retirement, and 7% annual growth over 30 years. First, the growth: $10,000 × 1.07^30 = **$76,122.55**.

**Traditional 401(k):** you invest the full $10,000 pre-tax. It grows to $76,122.55, then you pay 22% tax at withdrawal: $76,122.55 × 0.78 = **$59,375.59** in your pocket.

**Roth 401(k):** you pay 22% tax now, so only $7,800 goes in. It grows tax-free: $7,800 × 7.612255 = **$59,375.59** in your pocket.

Identical. Commutative property of multiplication: it does not matter whether the tax haircut happens before or after the growth, as long as the rate is the same. ## When Roth wins: your retirement tax rate is higher

Now change one thing: you retire into a 25% bracket instead of 22%. The Roth number does not move, it is still **$59,375.59**, because you already paid your tax. The traditional balance gets hit harder at withdrawal: $76,122.55 × 0.75 = **$57,091.91**.

Roth wins by **$2,283.68** on a single $10,000 contribution. Stretch that across 30 years of contributions and the gap becomes life-changing money. Roth also wins on flexibility: qualified withdrawals are tax-free, and since 2024, Roth 401(k)s have no required minimum distributions, so the money can keep compounding untouched.

## When traditional wins: your retirement tax rate is lower

Flip it: you retire into a 12% bracket. The traditional balance now keeps far more: $76,122.55 × 0.88 = **$66,987.84**, while Roth still delivers $59,375.59. Traditional wins by **$7,612.26** on that same $10,000.

This is the most common real-world outcome. Most people earn less in retirement than at their peak, and the tax code's lower brackets do the rest. If you are in your peak earning years now, the upfront deduction at 22% or 24% is usually worth more than tax-free withdrawals later. ## The paycheck test: what $500 really costs you

This is the part a paycheck calculator makes concrete. Say you earn enough to sit in the 22% marginal bracket and you contribute $500 per paycheck:

| | Traditional 401(k) | Roth 401(k) |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution | $500 pre-tax | $500 after-tax |
| Tax saved today | $110 | $0 |
| Your take-home pay drops by | **$390** | **$500** |
| Amount actually invested | $500 | $500 |

The traditional contribution "costs" you only $390 of spending money because the $110 tax saving subsidizes it. The Roth costs the full $500. That $110 difference per paycheck is real money today, which is why traditional feels easier on a tight budget. But remember the tie math above: if your retirement tax rate ends up higher, the Roth's pain today buys you the win later.

## The 60-second decision

1. **Take the full employer match first, always.** A 50% match on your contributions is an instant 50% return. No tax debate beats free money. Contribute at least enough to capture all of it before optimizing anything else.
2. **Early career, lower income? Lean Roth.** If you are in the 10% or 12% bracket now, you are buying tax-free growth at the cheapest price you will ever get.
3. **Peak earning years? Lean traditional.** Deducting at 22% or 24% today and withdrawing at 12% or 15% later is the classic win.
4. **Expect a pension or big rental income in retirement? Lean Roth.** Income that fills your lower brackets in retirement pushes 401(k) withdrawals into higher ones.
5. **Not sure? Split it.** There is no rule against holding both. Hedging across tax treatments is a legitimate strategy when the future is uncertain.

Whichever you choose, the amount you save matters more than the account type. A 15% savings rate in the "wrong" account beats a 6% rate in the "right" one every time. ## FAQ

### Roth vs traditional 401(k): which is better?
Neither is universally better. At equal tax rates now and in retirement, both leave you with identical spendable money ($59,375.59 on a $10,000 contribution over 30 years at 7%). Roth wins if your retirement tax rate will be higher; traditional wins if it will be lower.

### Is Roth 401(k) better than traditional if I am young?
Usually yes. Young workers are typically in lower tax brackets, so they pay little tax now for decades of tax-free growth. Someone in the 12% bracket buying Roth is locking in a 12% lifetime tax rate on that money.

### Can I have both a Roth and traditional 401(k)?
Yes. Most employer plans let you split contributions between the two, and you can change the split anytime. This is a reasonable hedge if you are unsure about future tax rates.

### Do Roth 401(k)s have required minimum distributions?
No, not anymore. Starting in 2024, Roth 401(k)s are exempt from required minimum distributions, matching Roth IRAs. Traditional 401(k)s still require RMDs starting at age 73.

### What happens if I withdraw early from each?
Traditional 401(k) withdrawals before age 59 and a half generally face income tax plus a 10% penalty. Roth 401(k) contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn anytime tax-free and penalty-free, since you already paid tax on that money.
