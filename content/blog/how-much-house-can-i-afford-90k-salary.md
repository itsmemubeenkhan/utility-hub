$90,000 puts you comfortably above the US median household income, and it opens up most suburban markets in the country. If you are wondering **how much house can I afford making $90,000 a year**, the lending industry's standard **28/36 rule** puts the answer around **$310k–$345k**, depending on your debts, down payment, rate, and state. Here is the exact math.

## The 28/36 rule, step by step

Lenders use two ratios:

- **Front-end (28%):** Housing costs ≤ 28% of gross monthly income
- **Back-end (36%):** All monthly debts ≤ 36% of gross monthly income

On $90,000/year ($7,500/month):

- Front-end limit: $7,500 × 0.28 = **$2,100/month** for housing
- Back-end limit: $7,500 × 0.36 = $2,700/month for everything

If you have $550/month in car and student loan payments, the back-end rule allows $2,700 − $550 = $2,150 for housing, so the front-end $2,100 is the binding limit.

## Converting the payment to a price

At 6.5% on a 30-year loan, $2,100/month in principal and interest supports a loan of about **$332,000**. With 20% down, that means a home price of roughly **$415,000**, but wait. That $2,100 must also cover property taxes and insurance (PITI), which lenders include in "housing." After ~$360/month for taxes and insurance, the P&I budget is closer to $1,741, supporting a loan of ~$275,000 and a price of **~$345,000** with 20% down.

Realistic ranges on $90K salary:

| Down payment | Approx. max home price (6.5%, 30-yr) |
|---|---|
| 3% down (conventional) | ~$295,000 |
| 10% down | ~$310,000 |
| 20% down | ~$345,000 |
| Debts over $900/mo | subtract ~$50,000 |

## Five things that move the number

1. **Interest rate.** At 5.5% instead of 6.5%, the same payment buys ~12% more house - roughly $38,000 extra at this income. Rate shopping is the highest-leverage move you can make.
2. **Existing debts.** Every $200/month in debt payments cuts roughly $30,000 off your max price. Killing a car payment before buying is powerful - that $400/month car note is costing you $60,000 in buying power.
3. **Down payment.** At this income, 20% down is within reach for most buyers within a few years of saving - and it removes PMI (about 0.5–1% of the loan per year, or $115–$230/month here) while lowering the monthly payment from day one.
4. **Property taxes.** In New Jersey or Illinois, taxes can add $700+/month on homes in this range; in Texas, expect ~$500–$600; in Alabama or Colorado, under $200. Location changes affordability enormously.
5. **HOA dues.** A $300/month HOA fee counts against your ratios exactly like a higher mortgage payment - on a townhome or condo, verify this before falling in love with the listing.

## The costs buyers forget

The mortgage is only the headline. Budget ~1% of the home's value per year for maintenance ($3,450/year on a $345,000 home), plus utilities (often $200–$350/month for a house), and an emergency fund that covers 3–6 months of the *new* higher housing cost, not your old rent. At this price point, don't skip the home inspection to save $500 - it is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

## Should you max it out?

The 28/36 rule is a **ceiling**, not a target. At $90K, buying around $280K–$310K instead of maxing to $345K leaves real room for savings, investing, and life. Being "house poor" - technically approved but with zero margin - is how a good salary turns into chronic financial stress. The buyers who sleep best at this income keep housing near 25% of gross.

## State-by-state: Texas, Florida, California

**Texas ($90K salary):** No state income tax helps, but property taxes at 1.6–2.2% bite hard - $415–$570/month on a $310K home. Realistic range: **$295K–$325K**. Your dollar goes far in Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio suburbs.

**Florida ($90K salary):** No state income tax, property tax ~0.9% (~$230/month on $310K), but homeowners insurance keeps climbing - budget $200–$350/month. Realistic range: **$300K–$335K**. Insurance quotes first, offer second.

**California ($90K salary):** State income tax ~8% at this level, and $90K is a stretch for single-family homes in coastal metros (median $700K+). Realistic range: **$310K–$345K** on paper - in practice, a condo inland, or a dual-income purchase for a house near job centers.

## FAQ

### How much house can I afford making $90,000 a year?
On a $90,000 salary with typical debts and 10–20% down, you can afford a home priced around **$310,000–$345,000** at current rates, using the 28/36 lending rule. That assumes roughly $2,100/month for total housing costs.

### What mortgage payment can I afford on $90K salary?
About **$2,100/month** for all housing costs (mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA) under the 28% front-end rule, assuming your total debts stay under 36% of income ($2,700/month).

### Can I afford a $350K house on a $90K salary?
It is tight but possible with 20% down ($70,000) and low other debts. With 10% down at 6.5%, a $350K home runs about $2,280/month in PITI - slightly over the 28% guideline. A lower rate or bigger down payment makes it work.

### Is $90K a good salary to buy a house in 2026?
Yes, in most US markets. $90K buys a solid 3-bedroom home in the South, Midwest, and suburbs nationwide. In high-cost coastal metros, it typically means a condo or townhome, or buying with a partner.

### How much house can I afford on $90K salary in Texas?
Texas has no state income tax but high property taxes (1.6–2.2%). Realistically **$295K–$325K** with 10–20% down - strong buying power in Texas metros at this income.
