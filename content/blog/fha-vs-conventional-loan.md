First-time buyers hear "FHA" and "conventional" thrown around like everyone knows the difference. Here is the plain-English breakdown, including the cost traps most articles gloss over.

## The quick comparison

| | FHA loan | Conventional loan |
|---|---|---|
| Backed by | Federal Housing Administration | Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac (private lenders) |
| Min down payment | 3.5% (with 580+ credit) | 3–5% (some programs 3%) |
| Min credit score | 580 (500 with 10% down) | 620 |
| Mortgage insurance | MIP (upfront + annual, often for life of loan) | PMI (drops at 20% equity) |
| Loan limits | Lower (varies by county) | Higher |
| Property rules | Must be primary residence; stricter appraisal standards | Primary, second home, or investment |

## When FHA wins

- **Low credit score (580–620):** FHA is often the only approval path. Conventional lenders price low scores punitively or decline outright.
- **Small down payment with weaker credit:** 3.5% down at 580 is FHA's signature advantage.
- **Higher DTI:** FHA tolerates back-end ratios up to 50%+ with compensating factors; conventional usually caps around 45%.

## The MIP trap most buyers miss

Here is what changes the math: FHA's mortgage insurance premium (MIP) has two parts, an **upfront fee** (1.75% of the loan, usually rolled into the balance) and an **annual premium** (around 0.55% for most borrowers) — and for most FHA loans originated today, **the annual MIP lasts for the entire life of the loan**. You cannot drop it at 20% equity like conventional PMI.

On a $350,000 loan, that is roughly $160/month in MIP that never goes away unless you refinance into a conventional loan later. Over 10 years, that is over $19,000 in insurance premiums alone.

Conventional PMI, by contrast, **automatically terminates at 78% loan-to-value** and can be removed at 80% on request. For a buyer who will build equity, conventional is usually cheaper long-term.

## When conventional wins

- **Credit score 680+:** You will get competitive pricing and droppable PMI.
- **20% down available:** No mortgage insurance at all. This is always the cheapest path.
- **Buying above FHA loan limits:** In high-cost areas, FHA caps may not cover the home you need.
- **Investment or second home:** FHA is primary-residence only.

## The smart first-timer strategy

If your credit is the barrier, consider this path: **buy with FHA now, refinance to conventional later** once your score is 700+ and you have 20% equity. Sitting at 650? See what a [650 credit score means for buying a house](/blog/is-650-credit-score-good-for-buying-a-house) — FHA is often the best loan type at that score. You get the home sooner with FHA's lenient approval, then escape the lifetime MIP with a refinance. Just make sure the numbers work: refinancing costs 2–5% of the loan in closing costs, so run the breakeven.

## The bottom line

FHA = easier approval, lifetime insurance cost. Conventional = harder approval, cheaper long-term. If you qualify for both, conventional almost always wins on total cost.
