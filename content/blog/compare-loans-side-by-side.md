Two loan offers can look almost identical and cost thousands of dollars differently. The way to **compare loans side by side** is simple: line up four numbers for each offer, and judge the winner by **total cost**, not the monthly payment. Below is a real $20,000 example where the "cheaper-looking" loan costs $1,698 more.

## The 4 numbers that matter

For every offer, write down:

1. **APR** (not just the interest rate - APR folds in fees, so it is the fairer comparison)
2. **Term** (months or years - this decides how long interest keeps running)
3. **Monthly payment** (what leaves your account)
4. **Total cost** (monthly payment × term + upfront fees)

The loan with the lowest monthly payment is very often the *most expensive* one, because a longer term keeps interest compounding longer. Always compute total cost last and let it decide.

## Worked example: $20,000, two offers

**Offer A:** 7.5% APR, 48 months. **Offer B:** 9.0% APR, 60 months. Same principal, both fixed-rate.

| | Offer A (7.5%, 4 yrs) | Offer B (9.0%, 5 yrs) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment | $483.58 | $415.17 |
| Total paid | $23,211.75 | $24,910.03 |
| Total interest | $3,211.75 | $4,910.03 |

Offer B's payment is $68/month lower, which is why it gets picked. But side by side, Offer B costs **$1,698.28 more in interest**. That lower payment is just you renting the money for an extra year at a higher rate. When you compare loans side by side like this, the winner is obvious: Offer A, and it is not close.

## Same term, different rate: what 1% of APR is worth

Rate still matters a lot when the term is equal. On a **$30,000 loan over 5 years**:

| | 6.5% APR | 8.5% APR |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment | $586.98 | $615.50 |
| Total interest | $5,219.07 | $6,929.76 |

A 2-point APR gap costs **$1,710.69** - about **$855 per point of APR** on this loan size and term. That is why rate shopping beats payment shopping: negotiating the rate down a single point saves real money, while picking the "lower payment" without checking the total can *add* thousands. If you only remember one thing from this page: compare the APR at the same term, then compare the totals. More on the distinction in [APR vs APY: the difference](/blog/apr-vs-apy-difference).

## The fee trap: 7.5% can lose to 8%

APR is supposed to include fees, but not every quote you get is an APR. Watch for:

- **Origination fees:** 2% on a $20,000 loan is $400, either paid upfront or rolled into the balance. An offer at "7.5% + 2% origination" can quietly cost more than a clean "8.0% with no fees".
- **Prepayment penalties:** some lenders charge you for paying early. If you plan extra payments, this erases the benefit.
- **Discount points:** on mortgages, each point costs 1% of the loan to buy the rate down - only worth it if you keep the loan long enough to break even.

When quotes mix APR and plain interest rate, convert everything to total cost yourself: monthly payment × term + fees. That is the only number lenders cannot dress up.

## The extra-payment test

Once you have picked the cheaper offer, check how fast you could kill it. Add $100/month to Offer A above ($483.58 → $583.58): the loan clears in **39 months instead of 48** and total interest drops to about **$2,578**, saving roughly **$634** more. Every extra dollar goes straight to principal and shrinks every future interest charge. If you are juggling debts, pair this with [debt snowball vs avalanche](/blog/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche) to decide which balance the extra money hits first.

## A 60-second comparison checklist

1. Get both quotes in writing: APR, term, payment, all fees.
2. Multiply payment × term, add fees: that is each offer's total cost.
3. Pick the lower total cost, not the lower payment.
4. Check prepayment terms and the break-even on any points or fees.
5. Model your real offer in the [loan comparison calculator](/tools/loan-comparison-calculator) before signing.

## FAQ

### How do I compare loans side by side?
Write down APR, term, monthly payment and total cost for each offer, then compare totals. The lowest total cost (payment × term + fees) is the cheapest loan, even if its monthly payment is higher. Our [loan comparison calculator](/tools/loan-comparison-calculator) does this in one screen.

### Is the loan with the lowest monthly payment the cheapest?
Usually not. In the example above, a $20,000 loan at 9.0% over 60 months has a $415.17 payment versus $483.58 for the 7.5% / 48-month offer, but costs **$1,698 more in total interest**. Lower payments typically mean longer terms and more interest.

### Should I compare APR or interest rate?
Compare **APR** when the terms are equal, because APR includes lender fees and is the fairer number. If a quote only shows the plain interest rate, add the fees yourself: total cost = payment × term + fees. Read [APR vs APY](/blog/apr-vs-apy-difference) for the full distinction.

### How much does 1% of APR change the cost?
On a $30,000 loan over 5 years, each point of APR is worth about **$855** in total interest (6.5% vs 8.5% is a $1,711 difference). On bigger balances or longer terms the number grows fast - always compute your exact figures instead of guessing.

### Do origination fees matter when comparing loans?
Yes. A 2% origination fee on $20,000 is $400 paid upfront or added to the balance, which can make a "7.5% + 2% fee" offer more expensive than a clean "8.0% no-fee" offer. Always add fees to the total before comparing.
