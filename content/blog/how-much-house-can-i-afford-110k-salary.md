$110,000 puts you comfortably above the US median household income - this is solid upper-middle-class buying power in most of the country. If you are wondering **how much house can I afford making $110,000 a year**, the lending industry's standard **28/36 rule** puts the answer around **$375k–$420k**, depending on your debts, down payment, rate, and state. Here is the exact math.

## The 28/36 rule, step by step

Lenders use two ratios:

- **Front-end (28%):** Housing costs ≤ 28% of gross monthly income
- **Back-end (36%):** All monthly debts ≤ 36% of gross monthly income

On $110,000/year ($9,167/month):

- Front-end limit: $9,167 × 0.28 = **$2,567/month** for housing
- Back-end limit: $9,167 × 0.36 = $3,300/month for everything

If you have $600/month in car and student loan payments, the back-end rule allows $3,300 − $600 = $2,700 for housing, so the front-end $2,567 is the binding limit.

## Converting the payment to a price

At 6.5% on a 30-year loan, $2,567/month in principal and interest supports a loan of about **$406,000**. With 20% down, that means a home price of roughly **$508,000**, but wait. That $2,567 must also cover property taxes and insurance (PITI), which lenders include in "housing." After ~$440/month for taxes and insurance, the P&I budget is closer to $2,127, supporting a loan of ~$337,000 and a price of **~$420,000** with 20% down.

Realistic ranges on $110K salary:

| Down payment | Approx. max home price (6.5%, 30-yr) |
|---|---|
| 3% down (conventional) | ~$360,000 |
| 10% down | ~$375,000 |
| 20% down | ~$420,000 |
| Debts over $1,000/mo | subtract ~$65,000 |

## Five things that move the number

1. **Interest rate.** At 5.5% instead of 6.5%, the same payment buys ~12% more house - that is roughly $45,000 extra at this income level. Rate shopping matters enormously.
2. **Existing debts.** Every $200/month in debt payments cuts roughly $30,000 off your max price. At $110K income, clearing a $500 car payment before buying can add $75,000+ to your budget.
3. **Down payment.** 20% avoids PMI (about 0.5–1% of the loan per year, or $140–$280/month here) and directly raises the price you can reach. On $110K, saving 20% is realistic within 2–3 years of disciplined saving.
4. **Property taxes.** In New Jersey or Illinois, taxes can add $800+/month on a $400K home; in Alabama or Colorado, under $200. Location changes affordability enormously.
5. **HOA dues.** A $350/month HOA fee counts against your ratios exactly like a higher mortgage payment - on a condo, this can cut $50,000+ off your max price.

## The costs buyers forget

The mortgage is only the headline. Budget ~1% of the home's value per year for maintenance ($4,000/year on a $400,000 home), plus utilities (often $250–$400/month for a house vs. an apartment), and an emergency fund that covers 3–6 months of the *new* higher housing cost, not your old rent. At this price point, a roof replacement or HVAC failure can cost $10,000–$20,000 - the emergency fund is not optional.

## Should you max it out?

The 28/36 rule is a **ceiling**, not a target. At $110K, buying 10–20% below your max ($340K–$380K instead of $420K) leaves serious room for savings, investing, and life. Being "house poor" at six figures - technically approved but with zero margin - is how a good salary turns into chronic financial stress. The happiest buyers at this income tend to land around $350K–$380K.

## State-by-state: Texas, Florida, California

**Texas ($110K salary):** No state income tax helps your take-home, but property taxes run 1.6–2.2% - on a $400K home, that is $530–$730/month just in taxes. Realistic range: **$360K–$400K**. The tax bite roughly cancels the income-tax savings.

**Florida ($110K salary):** No state income tax, property tax ~0.9% ($300/month on $400K), but homeowners insurance has exploded - budget $250–$450/month in many counties (hurricane risk). Realistic range: **$370K–$410K**. Get insurance quotes *before* making an offer.

**California ($110K salary):** State income tax takes ~9% at this level, and while Prop 13 keeps property tax ~0.75%, median home prices ($750K+ in metros) dwarf what $110K supports. Realistic range: **$375K–$420K** on paper, but in practice this buys a condo inland or requires a dual income for a single-family home in job centers.

## FAQ

### How much house can I afford making $110,000 a year?
On a $110,000 salary with typical debts and 10–20% down, you can afford a home priced around **$375,000–$420,000** at current rates, using the 28/36 lending rule. That assumes roughly $2,567/month for total housing costs.

### What mortgage payment can I afford on $110K salary?
About **$2,567/month** for all housing costs (mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA) under the 28% front-end rule, assuming your total debts stay under 36% of income ($3,300/month).

### Can I afford a $450K house on a $110K salary?
It is a stretch. You would need 20%+ down ($90,000), minimal other debts, or a below-market rate. With 10% down at 6.5%, a $450K home costs about $2,950/month in PITI - over the 28% guideline for $110K income.

### Is $110K a good salary to buy a house in 2026?
Yes, in most US markets. $110K buys a comfortable single-family home in the South, Midwest, and suburbs nationwide. In high-cost coastal metros (San Francisco, New York City, Los Angeles, San Diego), it usually means a condo, townhome, or a dual-income household for a house.

### How much house can I afford on $110K salary in Texas?
Texas has no state income tax but property taxes run 1.6–2.2%. Realistically **$360K–$400K** with 10–20% down, slightly below the national range for this income.

### How much do I need for a down payment on a $400K house?
20% is $80,000 (avoids PMI); 10% is $40,000 (PMI adds ~$130–$200/month); 3% conventional is $12,000 (PMI higher, ~$200–$300/month). Use our [home affordability calculator](/tools/home-affordability-calculator) to compare scenarios.
