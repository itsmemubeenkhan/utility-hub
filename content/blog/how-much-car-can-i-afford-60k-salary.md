Car dealers will happily sell you a $45,000 truck on a $60,000 salary. Your budget will not be happy about it. If you are asking **how much car can I afford on a $60K salary** - or, put plainly, how much should I spend on a car if I make $60,000 a year - the honest answer is roughly **$14,000–$18,000**, bought with 20% down and a 4-year loan. Here is how to calculate what you can actually afford before you walk onto the lot.

## The rules that keep you safe

Two guidelines dominate car affordability advice:

**The 15% rule:** Spend no more than **15% of your monthly take-home pay** on total car costs, payment plus insurance plus fuel. On $60,000 salary, take-home is roughly $3,750–$4,000/month after taxes (varies by state), so 15% gives you **$560–$600/month** for everything car-related.

**The 20/4/10 rule:** Put **20% down**, finance for no more than **4 years**, and keep total car expenses under **10% of gross income**. On $60,000, that is $500/month max for the payment itself.

Both rules point to the same neighborhood. Use the stricter one as your ceiling.

## What that buys you

Working backward from a $550/month all-in budget:

- Insurance on a financed car: ~$150–$200/month for most drivers
- Fuel and maintenance: ~$100–$150/month
- **Leftover for the payment: ~$250–$300/month**

At 7% APR over 48 months (the 20/4/10 term limit), a $275/month payment supports roughly an **$11,500 loan**. With 20% down, that means a car priced around **$14,000–$15,000**.

That number shocks people, and it should. It is the *responsible* number. Stretching to a 72-month term to "afford" a $25,000 car is exactly how people end up underwater, owing more than the car is worth for years.

## The 72-month trap

Dealers love the 72-month (or 84-month) loan because it makes an expensive car look affordable. The math says otherwise. On a **$25,000 car at 7% APR**:

- **48 months:** $598.66/month, total paid $28,735.49 (interest $3,735.49)
- **72 months:** $426.23/month, total paid $30,688.21 (interest $5,688.21)

The 72-month loan "saves" you $172 a month but costs **$1,952.72 more in interest**, and you stay underwater far longer - new cars lose about 20% of their value in year one, so a 6-year loan on a depreciating car is a recipe for owing more than it is worth until year four or five. The 4-year cap in the 20/4/10 rule exists precisely to stop this.

## The costs everyone forgets

The payment is only part of ownership. Budget for all of it:

- **Insurance:** Required to be comprehensive on financed cars, often double the cost of liability-only.
- **Depreciation:** New cars lose roughly 20% in year one. A 2–3 year old car dodges the steepest drop.
- **Maintenance and repairs:** Budget $100/month minimum; tires, brakes, and surprises are not optional.
- **Registration and taxes:** Varies by state but can add thousands upfront.

## New vs used on $60k

On this salary, **a 2–4 year old used car is almost always the right answer**. You skip the worst depreciation, insurance is cheaper, and the payment fits the rules above. Buy new only if you can pay cash or the payment genuinely fits inside the 15% rule, which on $60,000 it rarely does for new cars.

## The bottom line

On $60,000, target roughly **$14,000–$18,000** for the car price with 20% down and a 4-year loan. It is not glamorous, but neither is a $600/month payment strangling your ability to save, invest, or handle emergencies. ### What car can I afford making $60,000 a year?
Making $60,000 a year, aim for a **$14,000–$18,000 car** with 20% down and a 48-month loan at a competitive rate. After insurance (~$150–$200/month) and fuel (~$100–$150/month), that leaves about $250–$300/month for the payment itself, which the rules above can support.

### How much should I spend on a car if I make $60,000 a year?

About **$250–$350/month** for the payment itself. ### Is $60K salary enough to buy a new car?
Usually not without breaking the affordability rules. The average new car costs well over $40,000, and even a modest $28,000 new car at 7% over 4 years is a $670/month payment before insurance - over the $500–$600 ceiling on $60K. A **2–4 year old used car** is the smart move on this salary: less depreciation, cheaper insurance, and a payment that fits.

### Should I get a 72-month loan to afford a more expensive car?
No. On a $25,000 car at 7%, a 72-month loan costs **$1,952.72 more in interest** than a 48-month loan ($5,688 vs $3,735) and keeps you underwater longer as the car depreciates. The 20/4/10 rule caps financing at 4 years for exactly this reason - if the car only fits your budget on a 6- or 7-year loan, it does not fit your budget.
