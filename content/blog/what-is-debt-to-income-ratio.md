Your debt-to-income ratio, DTI, is the number lenders care about almost as much as your credit score. It is simple math with outsized consequences: it can be the difference between approval and rejection, or between a good rate and an expensive one.

## What DTI actually measures

DTI is your **total monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income**, expressed as a percentage.

> DTI = (Monthly debt payments ÷ Gross monthly income) × 100

Monthly debts include: mortgage or rent, car loans, student loans, minimum credit card payments, child support, and any other required monthly obligations. It does **not** include utilities, groceries, insurance premiums, or taxes, only debt payments.

Example: $2,800 in monthly debts ÷ $8,000 gross monthly income = **35% DTI**.

## The two DTIs lenders use

Mortgage lenders actually look at two ratios:

- **Front-end ratio:** Housing costs only (mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA) ÷ income. Lenders typically want this under **28%**.
- **Back-end ratio:** All monthly debts ÷ income. Conventional loans usually cap this at **36–45%**; FHA allows up to **50–57%** in some cases with compensating factors.

This is the famous **28/36 rule**: spend no more than 28% of income on housing and 36% on all debts. It is a guideline, not a law, but it is the backbone of mortgage underwriting.

## What counts as "good"

- **Under 20%:** Excellent. You have tons of borrowing capacity.
- **20–35%:** Healthy. Most lenders will be happy.
- **36–43%:** The caution zone for conventional mortgages. Still approvable, often with a good credit score.
- **44–50%:** Difficult for conventional; FHA or other programs may work with strong compensating factors (big down payment, cash reserves, excellent credit).
- **Over 50%:** Very hard to get new credit. Time to pay down debt first.

## How to lower your DTI before applying

You can attack either side of the fraction:

**Reduce the debts (numerator):**
- Pay off small loans entirely, eliminating a $200/month payment moves the needle more than partially paying a large one.
- Pay down credit cards to reduce minimum payments (note: minimums are what count, not balances).
- Avoid new debt completely in the 6–12 months before applying.

**Increase the income (denominator):**
- Lenders use documented, stable income: salary, consistent bonuses, verified side income with a 2-year history.
- A raise or a working spouse's income counts once documented.

**Do not:** close old accounts (hurts credit age), take on new debt, or make large undocumented deposits that trigger underwriting questions.

## DTI is not just for mortgages

Auto lenders, landlords, and personal loan underwriters all use DTI variants. Even if you are not buying a house, keeping DTI under 35% means you are living within your means with room for surprises.

## The bottom line

Calculate both your front-end and back-end ratios before you talk to any lender. If you are over the thresholds, you now know exactly how much debt to clear or income to document.
