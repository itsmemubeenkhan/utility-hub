The Roth vs Traditional IRA question trips up almost everyone, because both are called "IRAs" but they treat your taxes in opposite ways. Get this choice right and you can save yourself thousands in lifetime taxes. Get it wrong and there is no undo button, though you do get to revisit the decision every year.

## The core difference: when you pay tax

A **Traditional IRA** gives you a tax deduction now. Contribute $7,000 (the 2026 limit for most people under 50), and if you are eligible, you subtract that $7,000 from this year's taxable income. The money grows tax-deferred, and you pay ordinary income tax on withdrawals in retirement.

A **Roth IRA** gives you no deduction now. You contribute after-tax dollars, the money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are **completely tax-free**, including all the growth.

So the entire decision comes down to one bet: **will your tax rate be higher now or in retirement?** If you expect to be in a higher bracket later, Roth wins. If you expect a lower rate later, Traditional wins.

## The simple framework most people should use

**Choose Roth if:**
- You are early in your career and in a lower tax bracket (say, 12% or 22% federal). Paying tax now at a low rate to get decades of tax-free growth is usually the winning trade.
- You expect your income to rise significantly over your career.
- You want flexibility: Roth contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn anytime without tax or penalty, which makes it a decent backup emergency layer.

**Choose Traditional if:**
- You are in your peak earning years (32% bracket or higher). The deduction now is worth more than tax-free withdrawals later, especially if you will retire into a lower bracket.
- You need the deduction to qualify for other tax benefits, or you are close to an income threshold.
- Your employer offers no retirement plan and you want every dollar of tax relief you can get today.

**The honest answer for most young savers:** Roth. Time is the Roth's superpower. A 25-year-old contributing $7,000 a year at a 7% average return would have roughly $1.5 million by 65, and with a Roth, all of it, contributions plus growth, comes out tax-free. That is hard to beat.

## Income limits and the fine print

Both IRAs have eligibility rules that change yearly, so verify current limits before contributing:

- **Roth IRA:** Direct contributions phase out above certain incomes (for 2026, roughly $150,000–$165,000 single, $236,000–$246,000 married filing jointly, check the IRS for exact figures). Earn too much and you need the "backdoor Roth" strategy instead.
- **Traditional IRA:** Anyone with earned income can contribute, but the *deduction* phases out if you or your spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan and your income exceeds thresholds.
- **Withdrawals:** Traditional IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally face a 10% penalty plus income tax (with exceptions). Roth earnings withdrawn before 59½ and before the account is 5 years old face tax and penalty too, though your original contributions always come out free.

## Can you have both?

Yes, and many people should. The $7,000 annual limit applies across both combined, not per account, so you might put $4,000 in Roth and $3,000 in Traditional, for example. Splitting gives you **tax diversification**: some money taxed now, some later, so you can manage your taxable income strategically in retirement.

## The bottom line

If you are young or in a low bracket, lean Roth. If you are in your peak earning years, lean Traditional. If you are unsure, splitting is a perfectly respectable answer. The biggest mistake is not the Roth-vs-Traditional choice, it is contributing to neither.
