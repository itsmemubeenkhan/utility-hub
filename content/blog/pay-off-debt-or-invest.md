This is one of the most argued questions in personal finance, and most of the argument is unnecessary. There is a clean mathematical answer, plus a short list of human exceptions. Here is both.

## The mathematical answer: compare rates

It comes down to one comparison: **your debt's interest rate versus your expected investment return.**

- **Debt above ~7–8%? Pay it off first.** Credit cards at 20%+ are an emergency, not a debate. No investment reliably beats a guaranteed 22% return, which is exactly what paying off a 22% card gives you.
- **Debt below ~4–5%? Invest first.** A mortgage at 6% or a student loan at 4% costs less than the stock market's long-run average return (roughly 7–10% before inflation, 7% after, depending on the period you measure). Investing the extra dollars usually wins mathematically.
- **Debt at 5–7%? The gray zone.** The math is close enough that either choice is defensible. This is where the human factors below decide.

The key word is *expected*. Investment returns are uncertain; debt payoff returns are guaranteed. A guaranteed 6% (paying off a 6% loan) beats an *expected* 8% with real risk of being 2% for a stretch of years. Adjust the framework for your risk tolerance: conservative people should use a lower threshold, maybe 5–6%, before investing wins.

## The non-negotiable order of operations

Before the debt-vs-invest debate even starts, handle these in order:

1. **Minimum payments on everything.** Never miss minimums, late fees and credit damage cost more than any optimization.
2. **A small emergency buffer.** $1,000–$2,000 in savings, so one car repair does not become new credit card debt.
3. **Employer 401(k) match.** If your job matches contributions, contribute enough to get the full match *even while paying off high-interest debt*. A 50–100% instant return beats everything.
4. **High-interest debt** (credit cards, payday loans, anything over ~8%).
5. **Then** the debt-vs-invest decision for the remaining moderate-rate debt.

## The human exceptions

Math is not the whole story:

- **Sleep-at-night factor.** Some people cannot stand any debt. If being debt-free is worth a slightly lower net worth to you, that is a valid preference, not a mistake.
- **Cash flow freedom.** Paying off a $300/month car loan frees $300/month of flexibility even if investing would have earned slightly more. Flexibility has real value.
- **Upcoming mortgage application.** Lenders care about your debt-to-income ratio. Paying down debts before applying can get you approved or get you a better rate, which dwarfs investment returns.
- **Tax nuances.** Mortgage interest may be deductible; 401(k) contributions reduce taxable income. These tilt the math modestly toward investing in tax-advantaged accounts.

## What about "doing both"?

Splitting extra money between debt payoff and investing is the most popular answer and usually the worst of both worlds in the gray zone: you get neither the guaranteed return of aggressive payoff nor the full compounding of aggressive investing. Pick a lane based on the rate comparison above, with one exception: always keep getting the employer match.

## The bottom line

Above 8%: kill the debt. Below 5%: invest. Between: follow your gut, but never skip the employer match.
